# Asset Playbook

Use this reference when deciding what an asset should feel like and how it should be composed.

## 1. Define the communication job

Before touching layout, answer these questions:

- What is the asset trying to make the viewer do or feel?
- Who is the audience?
- Where will it be seen: print, slide deck, README, website, social feed, event screen, email, or video thumbnail?
- What information is mandatory?
- What is allowed to be decorative versus purely functional?

If the brief is fuzzy, reduce it to one sentence:

- "Make a warm poster that helps families notice the school play."
- "Make a government notice that feels trustworthy and immediately readable."
- "Make a birthday card that feels personal, celebratory, and specific to the recipient."
- "Make a launch slide that communicates one technical idea fast."

## 2. Match the style to the context

Use tone intentionally.

### Children’s play

- Use warmer or brighter palettes.
- Use larger type and clearer shapes.
- Let illustration, whimsy, and motion cues do more work.
- Keep the feeling welcoming and legible from a distance.

### Government or public-service poster

- Use high contrast and restrained decoration.
- Use a rigid hierarchy and obvious spacing.
- Prefer clarity, trust, and readability over novelty.
- Use icons and panels only if they reduce ambiguity.

### Birthday card or personal greeting

- Let personality lead.
- Use a more expressive palette or framing style.
- Make the central message emotionally clear.
- Keep copy short enough that the design still breathes.

### Slide or presentation visual

- Keep one message per frame.
- Remove non-essential ornament.
- Make the title and key visual readable at room distance.
- Treat every slide as a poster with less clutter.

### Launch or social asset

- Lead with one bold focal point.
- Use simple, memorable copy.
- Frame the subject tightly enough to survive small-screen viewing.
- Make the thumbnail strong even before the full text is read.

## 3. Build the visual system

Choose one answer for each layer before composing:

- Palette: muted, bright, editorial, institutional, playful, cinematic
- Typography: authoritative, friendly, technical, elegant, loud
- Shape language: rectangular, rounded, organic, angular, minimal
- Texture: clean, grainy, glossy, paper-like, luminous
- Framing: centered, split layout, grid, asymmetrical collage, full-bleed

Do not keep adding styles. Choose one system and make the whole asset obey it.

## 4. Create hierarchy on purpose

Every asset needs a reading order.

Typical hierarchy:

1. headline or central message
2. main image or focal subject
3. supporting details
4. metadata such as date, location, or CTA

If everything is loud, nothing is loud. Use size, contrast, weight, and spacing to show priority.

## 5. Choose a composition pattern

These patterns cover most jobs:

### Hero split

- Put text on one side and the main visual on the other.
- Use when the asset needs both explanation and atmosphere.

### Centered statement

- Put one strong image or background behind a centered message.
- Use when the message is short and emotional.

### Poster stack

- Stack headline, subhead, details, and CTA vertically.
- Use for events, public notices, and announcements.

### Collage grid

- Combine several related visuals in a structured grid.
- Use for capability overviews, showcases, or multi-scene storytelling.

### Feature card

- Place one media block inside a framed card with a small label and short caption.
- Use for social tiles, README sections, or supporting visuals around a hero.

### Reel poster

- Use one strong motion frame or frame strip, then add minimal text and an optional play cue.
- Use when the poster needs to promise motion without becoming cluttered.

## 6. Pick source media carefully

Choose source material by strength, not by fairness.

- Prefer the highest-resolution assets.
- Prefer images with one obvious focal point.
- Prefer frames that read well even when scaled down.
- Avoid near-duplicates unless the repetition is the point.
- Avoid sources that need the layout to rescue them.

If a source is weak, replace it rather than over-design around it.

## 7. Handle text like a design material

- Keep the headline short if possible.
- Break long text into deliberate lines rather than letting it wrap randomly.
- Avoid squeezing paragraphs into poster spaces.
- Use body copy only when the asset truly needs explanation.
- Let spacing do part of the work.

Good asset text feels edited, not merely present.

## 8. Review the asset

Run this pass before shipping:

- Zoom out. Does the main message still read?
- Squint. Does the hierarchy still hold?
- Check corners and edges. Is anything cramped or drifting off-balance?
- Check color contrast. Is every important word readable?
- Check crops. Are faces, subjects, or logos awkwardly cut?
- Check tone. Does the style fit the audience?
- Check honesty. Does the asset promise something the underlying work cannot support?

For motion assets:

- Check whether each clip earns its place.
- Check whether transitions feel intentional.
- Check whether the sequence varies enough to stay interesting.
- Check whether the reel is short enough to feel curated.
